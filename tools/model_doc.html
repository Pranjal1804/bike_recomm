Fig. 01 — System overview

Why six models?

No single algorithm captures all dimensions of a motorcycle purchase decision. Different models excel at different things: ranking by similarity to an ideal, weighting criteria through expert judgement, handling vague human preferences, finding similar profiles, and finally classifying whether a specific bike is likely to satisfy a particular rider. MotoMatch India combines five complementary ranking models in a weighted ensemble, and adds a sixth standalone binary classifier for compatibility checking.

Ensemble blend for the recommendation engine (Fig. 05 — "Find your machine"). The Logistic Regression model (Fig. 09) runs independently as a compatibility classifier.

Fig. 02 — Model 1 of 6

T
TOPSIS
Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution
25%Ensemble Weight

What it is

A multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA) method developed by Hwang & Yoon (1981). It ranks alternatives by their geometric distance from a hypothetical ideal solution and a hypothetical worst solution.

MCDADistance-based

What it does

For every bike, it computes two Euclidean distances in the weighted normalised criterion space: distance to the ideal best (D⁺) and to the ideal worst (D⁻). The closeness coefficient C = D⁻ / (D⁺ + D⁻) gives the final score.

Ci = Di⁻ / (Di⁺ + Di⁻)

Why it's used

It is the most robust and widely validated MCDA method. By measuring distance to both best and worst ideals, it avoids the flaw of simply picking the bike closest to the best alternative. It handles conflicting criteria gracefully.

  • NormalisationVector (L2)
  • Weights sourceAHP eigenvector
  • Handles tiesYes (geometric)

Step-by-step algorithm

StepOperationFormula
1Build decision matrix X (bikes × criteria)xij = criterion score for bike i, criterion j
2Vector normalise each columnrij = xij / √(Σ xkj²)
3Apply AHP weightsvij = wj · rij
4Determine ideal best A⁺ and worst A⁻A⁺ = max(vij), A⁻ = min(vij) per column
5Compute separation measuresDi⁺ = √Σ(vij−A⁺j)², likewise Di⁻
6Rank by closeness coefficientCi = Di⁻ / (Di⁺ + Di⁻) ∈ [0,1]

Fig. 03 — Model 2 of 6

A
AHP
Analytic Hierarchy Process
25%Ensemble Weight

What it is

A structured technique for organising and analysing complex decisions, developed by Thomas Saaty (1980). It decomposes a decision into a hierarchy and uses pairwise comparisons to derive relative criterion weights.

PairwiseEigenvectorWeight derivation

What it does

An 8×8 pairwise comparison matrix is constructed where each entry aij = importance of criterion i over j on Saaty's 1–9 scale. The principal eigenvector of this matrix gives the normalised criterion weights used by TOPSIS and WSM.

w = principal eigenvector of A
CR = CI / RI < 0.10 ✓

Why it's used

AHP provides mathematically grounded, transparent weights. Rather than assigning weights arbitrarily, it derives them from explicit expert pairwise judgements and verifies consistency via the Consistency Ratio (CR). If CR ≥ 0.10 the matrix must be revised.

  • Matrix size8 × 8
  • ScaleSaaty 1–9
  • CR target< 0.10

Fig. 04 — Model 3 of 6

F
Fuzzy Logic
Fuzzy Inference System with Trapezoidal & Triangular Membership Functions
20%Ensemble Weight

What it is

A rule-based system operating on fuzzy sets rather than crisp Boolean logic. Introduced by Zadeh (1965), it allows partial membership — a bike can be "somewhat within budget" rather than strictly in or out.

MamdaniDefuzzification

What it does

Six fuzzy rules fire for each bike (budget, power, mileage, comfort, experience, purpose). Trapezoidal and triangular membership functions convert crisp differences into fuzzy degrees of fit. A weighted centroid defuzzifies these into a single crisp score.

μ_trap(x; a,b,c,d):
0 if x<a; (x−a)/(b−a) if a≤x<b;
1 if b≤x≤c; (d−x)/(d−c) if c<x≤d

Why it's used

Human preferences are inherently vague — "I need good mileage" has no crisp boundary. Fuzzy logic captures linguistic hedges and gradual transitions that crisp scoring models miss. It's especially effective for comfort and budget proximity, where exact equality is rare.

  • MF typesTrapez. + Tri.
  • Defuzz.Weighted centroid
  • Rules6

Fig. 05 — Model 4 of 6

K
K-Nearest Neighbours
KNN Distance Similarity Scoring (k = 10)
15%Ensemble Weight

What it is

A non-parametric algorithm that classifies or ranks instances by measuring proximity to known reference points. In this usage, the user's preference profile is the "query" and each bike is a "data point" in normalised 7-D space.

Non-parametricEuclidean distance

What it does

Both the user preference vector and each bike's specification vector are normalised to [0, 1] across 7 dimensions (price, power, mileage, weight, comfort, experience, ground clearance). Euclidean distance is computed; score = 1 / (1 + dist).

dist = √Σ(uf − bf)²
score = 1 / (1 + dist) + purpose_bonus

Why it's used

KNN provides a holistic similarity score without any explicit scoring formula — it asks "is this bike generally close to what the rider described?" rather than evaluating each criterion in isolation. It catches good all-round bikes that may not excel on any single axis.

  • k value10
  • DistanceEuclidean (L2)
  • Dimensions7

Fig. 06 — Model 5 of 6

W
WSM
Weighted Sum Model (Simple Additive Weighting)
15%Ensemble Weight

What it is

The simplest and most interpretable MCDA technique. It calculates a composite score as a linear weighted sum of normalised criterion scores. Also known as Simple Additive Weighting (SAW).

LinearAdditiveInterpretable

What it does

Each bike gets a raw score on each of the 8 criteria (budget, power, mileage, comfort, experience, purpose, weight, GC). These are multiplied by AHP-derived weights and summed to produce a single scalar.

Si = Σ wj · sij
where sij ∈ [0, 1] is the criterion score

Why it's used

WSM acts as a sanity check and baseline. Because it is fully transparent, the team can audit results easily. It also helps balance the ensemble: when TOPSIS and Fuzzy disagree, WSM provides a stable reference score derived from the same AHP weights.

  • ComplexityO(n × m)
  • WeightsAHP eigenvector
  • Output range[0, 1]

Fig. 07 — Model 6 of 6 · Binary Classifier

LR
Logistic Regression
Binary Compatibility Classifier — "Will I like this bike?"
StandaloneClassifier

What it is

A supervised linear classifier that models the probability of a binary outcome. Here the outcome is: "would a rider with these preferences be satisfied by this specific bike?" The sigmoid function maps a linear score to a probability in (0, 1).

ClassificationSigmoid σ(z)Probability

What it does

It computes a dot-product of the θ weight vector with a 9-feature input vector built from the (rider, bike) pair, then applies the sigmoid. The output P ∈ (0,1) is interpreted as compatibility probability.

z = θ₀ + θ₁·f_budget + … + θ₉·f_gc
P(like) = σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z)

Why it's used

Unlike the ranking models, Logistic Regression gives a direct yes/no answer with a calibrated probability. It enables a fundamentally different query: not "rank all bikes" but "evaluate this specific bike for me." This is the Fig. 09 "Compatibility Check" feature of the platform.

  • Output typeProbability (0–1)
  • Features9 normalised
  • Thresholdσ(z) ≥ 0.58

Ensemble design rationale

The five ranking models are blended with the weights TOPSIS 25% + AHP 25% + Fuzzy 20% + K-NN 15% + WSM 15%. TOPSIS and AHP receive the highest weights because they are the most theoretically grounded. Fuzzy Logic receives 20% because human preference data is inherently imprecise. K-NN and WSM act as stabilisers and receive lower weights to prevent any individual model's quirks from dominating the ranking.

All five model scores are min-max normalised across the candidate field before blending, ensuring no model dominates simply due to a different output scale.

The Logistic Regression model sits entirely outside the ensemble. It is activated only when the user explicitly selects a specific bike and asks for a compatibility verdict — a fundamentally different query from "rank all bikes." Its θ weights were calibrated to reflect the same AHP criterion priorities and are verifiable in the platform's Fig. 09 result panel.
